# Glossary source candidates (draft)

Source index for the learning layer. Every URL below was opened from this machine with HTTP 200 and its visible text checked. Explanations are paraphrased. These are not yet recorded in `SOURCES.md`; a source enters `SOURCES.md` only when a value or sentence is used.

Terms covered: every row of `GLOSSARY_PLAN.md` with source status `pending`, plus "Where the thresholds come from" (ASCEND-2022 recorded, education page pending). Terms already recorded (NASA-TRL, CHALLENGE-RES, MARSHALL-SSN, ASCEND-2022) are not repeated.

### Mars and Earth orbits
- Source 1: https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/years-on-other-planets/en/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: How Long is a Year on Other Planets? | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Explains that a planet's year is the time it takes to orbit the Sun, with the Earth and Mars times given for comparison. Also says planets nearer the Sun orbit faster.
  - Location in page: Opening section, the list of planet orbit times, and the paragraph defining a year
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/lesson-plan/lets-go-to-mars-calculating-launch-windows/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Let's Go to Mars! Calculating Launch Windows – Math Lesson | NASA JPL Education
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Describes Earth and Mars as roughly circular orbits around the Sun, drawn with string and pushpins in a classroom activity. Written for high-school students, with simplifying assumptions stated.
  - Location in page: Background text and the activity steps that draw the two orbits
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: The kids page gives orbit times but no diagram of the two orbits. Ellipse shape of Mars's orbit is not explained on a kids page.

### Spacecraft and payload
- Source 1: https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/science/anatomy-of-a-spacecraft
  - Institution: ESA
  - Page title: Anatomy of a spacecraft
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Splits a spacecraft into the service module, which holds the engine, power and communications, and the payload module, which holds the science instruments. Says the payload depends on the mission goals.
  - Location in page: Paragraph on the essential components and the section "Science payload"
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/technology/science-payload/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Science Payload - NASA Science
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Lists the science instruments that form the Dawn mission payload, as an example of a payload in practice.
  - Location in page: "Science Payload" and "Science Instruments" sections
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids source uses the word payload. The spaceplace page "What Powers a Spacecraft?" describes instruments and power but not the term. ESA kids satellite page (payload definition) returned 404, so it is not used.

### Propellant (fuel)
- Source 1: https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/launching-into-space/en/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: How Do We Launch Things Into Space? | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Defines propellant as the fuel a rocket burns to make exhaust, and explains that a rocket needs enough propellant to beat gravity. Notes that heavier spacecraft need more propellant.
  - Location in page: "How does a rocket lift off?" and "Once a rocket launches, will it keep going?"
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter3-2/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Chapter 3: Gravity & Mechanics - NASA Science
  - Reading level: technical
  - Covers: Describes liquid and solid propellants used in rockets and how the propellant choice sets the exhaust material.
  - Location in page: Section on rocket propellant types (liquid and solid), within the propulsion text
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids page gives propellant mass numbers. Propellant mass fraction for a Mars trip is not explained at a kids level.

  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Chapter 3: Gravity & Mechanics - NASA Science
  - Reading level: technical

### Thrust and specific impulse
- Source 1: https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/launching-into-space/en/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: How Do We Launch Things Into Space? | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Defines thrust as the upward force from expelled exhaust, and says thrust must beat gravity to launch.
  - Location in page: "Once a rocket launches, will it keep going?" (definition of thrust)
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter3-2/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Chapter 3: Gravity & Mechanics - NASA Science
  - Reading level: technical
  - Covers: Defines specific impulse as thrust per unit of propellant weight flow, and says a higher value needs less propellant for the same change of momentum. Compares it to miles per gallon.
  - Location in page: Section on specific impulse, after the rocket equation
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: Specific impulse has no kids explanation. Engineers' units (seconds) are not explained for ages 10 to 14.

### Chemical and electric engine
- Source 1: https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/ion-balloons/en/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Ions in action | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Explains how an ion engine uses charged xenon ions pushed through a charged screen. Says the push is very small but the ions leave faster than hot gas from a regular rocket.
  - Location in page: "Ions in action" text and the section "The ion engine"
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dawn/technology/ion-propulsion/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Ion Propulsion - NASA Science
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Compares ion thrusters with chemical engines: ions leave at about 7 to 10 times the speed, and the engines use very little xenon for long periods.
  - Location in page: Paragraph on how the Dawn thrusters work
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids page gives the thrust-over-time trade-off (weak push, long burn) in one place. Chemical-engine burn times are not stated on a kids page.

  - Page title: Launch a rocket from a spinning planet | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Defines a launch window as an opening in time for the best launch, and explains that Earth and the target keep moving, so timing matters. Gives the example of using Earth's spin and orbit to save fuel.
  - Location in page: Section "Launch windows" and the text on aiming at a moving target
  - Verified: yes

  - Page title: Let's Go to Mars! Calculating Launch Windows – Math Lesson | NASA JPL Education
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Defines the launch window as the time span in which a launch must happen so that the spacecraft and Mars arrive together, and says it can last minutes or weeks.
  - Location in page: Background text on the launch window and the arrival of the spacecraft at Mars
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: The kids page says it was last updated more than 15 years ago; check figures before use. No Mars-specific kids page gives the two-year cycle with a diagram.

### Solar conjunction
- Source 1: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/whats-mars-solar-conjunction-and-why-does-it-matter/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: What's Mars Solar Conjunction, and Why Does It Matter? | NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Explains that solar conjunction happens when Mars and Earth are on opposite sides of the Sun. The Sun's gas then disturbs radio signals, so engineers hold back commands. It happens about every two years.
  - Location in page: Main article, paragraphs on the conjunction and the command pause
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://science.nasa.gov/resource/mars-solar-conjunction/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Mars Solar Conjunction - NASA Science
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Gives the same idea in short form, with an animation showing Mars behind the Sun and the Sun interrupting radio links.
  - Location in page: Description under the animation
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids page. Neither page gives the time length of a blackout in days.

### Radio bands S, X, Ka
- Source 1: https://www.nasa.gov/smallsat-institute/sst-soa/soa-communications/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: 9.0 Communications - NASA
  - Reading level: technical
  - Covers: Says NASA spacecraft use the S, X and Ka government bands. Says S-band is the usual band for tracking and commands, and that higher frequencies bring more atmospheric loss and rain fade.
  - Location in page: Communications overview and the paragraph on the NASA government bands
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://www.nasa.gov/general/ka-band-represents-the-future-of-space-communications/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: Ka-Band Represents the Future of Space Communications - NASA
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: Explains Ka-band as the newer band at 26 GHz, with data rates hundreds of times faster than S-band, and why the band is crowded at lower frequencies.
  - Location in page: Main article, paragraphs comparing Ka-band and S-band
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids source. X-band has no dedicated general page in this set. No thresholds linked to bands (thresholds are in ASCEND-2022).

### Solar array and sunlight at Mars
- Source 1: https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/what-powers-a-spacecraft/en/
  - Institution: NASA
  - Page title: What Powers a Spacecraft? | NASA Space Place – NASA Science for Kids
  - Reading level: kids
  - Covers: Explains that solar panels turn sunlight into electricity and that they get weaker farther from the Sun, so far spacecraft need bigger panels. Mars dust can cover panels and cut power.
  - Location in page: Sections "Solar power" and the paragraph on Mars dust and distance
  - Verified: yes
- Source 2: https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/Going_solar_for_interplanetary_missions
  - Institution: ESA
  - Page title: Going solar for interplanetary missions
  - Reading level: general
  - Covers: States the inverse square law: twice the distance gives a quarter of the sunlight and three times the distance gives a ninth. Mentions solar cells designed for low light.
  - Location in page: Paragraph on illumination falling with distance from the Sun
  - Verified: yes
- Gaps: No kids page gives the Mars-to-Sun sunlight number. Mars power figures are not given. Diagram idea (intensity falling with distance) can use the ESA rule.
